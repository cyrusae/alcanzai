# Sentence Structure: Formal

## Strategy
Employ academic register with complex sentence structures, passive voice where appropriate, and no contractions. Maintain scholarly tone suitable for formal academic writing.

**Philosophy**: Some contexts demand formal register—publications, dissertations, formal presentations. This register signals academic credibility and aligns with disciplinary conventions.

---

## When to Use This Register

✅ **Use Formal Structure when:**
- Preparing dissertation material
- Literature review for formal submissions
- Notes that may become publication text
- Writing for very traditional academic contexts
- Signaling expertise and credibility is important

❌ **Don't use Formal Structure when:**
- Personal reference notes (inefficient to read)
- Casual sharing with peers
- Want notes to feel accessible on re-reading
- Speed and efficiency outweigh formality

---

## Core Principles

### 1. No Contractions
Spell out all verb forms. Contractions undermine formal register.

**Formal**: "The authors do not provide explicit justification for this methodological choice, though it is consistent with established practice in the field."

**Not Formal**: "The authors don't provide explicit justification for this choice, though it's consistent with established practice."

### 2. Strategic Passive Voice Usage
Use passive voice when agent is unimportant, when emphasizing action over actor, or when following disciplinary conventions.

**Active voice (still 40-50%)**:
- "Smith argues that syntactic structure emerges from statistical regularities."
- "The researchers conducted 40 sociolinguistic interviews."
- "This finding challenges previous assumptions."

**Passive voice (50-60%)**:
- "Forty sociolinguistic interviews were conducted in Philadelphia."
- "The data were normalized using standard z-score transformation."
- "P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant."
- "The effect has been documented across multiple studies."

**Rule**: Use passive when it sounds more natural in formal academic prose, not to hide agency.

### 3. Complex Sentence Structures
Employ subordinate clauses, embedded structures, and sophisticated syntax. Show relationships between ideas within sentences.

**Simple**: "The model uses attention mechanisms. This enables parallel processing. The training time is reduced."

**Formal**: "The model employs attention mechanisms, which enable parallel processing and thereby substantially reduce training time compared to sequential architectures."

**Guidelines**:
- Average 20-30 words per sentence
- Multiple clauses per sentence common
- Use subordinating conjunctions: whereas, whereby, wherein, inasmuch as
- Embed explanatory material within sentences

### 4. Formal Academic Transitions
Use scholarly transition words and phrases that signal logical relationships explicitly.

**Formal transitions**:
- "Moreover" / "Furthermore" / "Additionally"
- "Conversely" / "In contrast" / "Alternatively"
- "Consequently" / "Therefore" / "Thus"
- "Nevertheless" / "Notwithstanding"
- "Specifically" / "Namely"
- "To this end" / "In this regard"
- "It should be noted that"

**Avoid casual transitions**:
- "But" (use "However")
- "So" (use "Therefore" or "Consequently")
- "Also" (use "Additionally" or "Moreover")

### 5. Third Person Exclusively
Maintain scholarly distance throughout.

**Formal**: "This analysis suggests...", "The present study demonstrates...", "The findings indicate that..."

**Not appropriate for synthesis**: "I argue that...", "One might conclude that..."

*Note: In original dissertation/publication writing, disciplinary conventions may permit "I argue" or strategic use of first person—but for synthesis of others' work, maintain third person throughout.*

## Examples Across Domains

### Linguistics/Phonetics
**Formal**:
"The present study investigates /æ/-tensing—characterized by raising and fronting of the low front vowel—in pre-nasal environments among Chicago English speakers. Acoustic analysis of F1 and F2 formant values reveals significant age stratification, whereby younger speakers exhibit substantially more tensing than their older counterparts (p < 0.001).

Data were obtained through 40 sociolinguistic interviews, which were subsequently coded for phonological environment, lexical class, and relevant social variables. Multivariate regression analysis controls for these factors while isolating the effect of speaker age. The observed patterns align with previous research on the Northern Cities Vowel Shift, though Chicago demonstrates more advanced backing of /ɑ/ relative to Detroit and Buffalo, suggesting regional variation in the trajectory of this sound change."

### Machine Learning
**Formal**:
"The transformer architecture represents a fundamental departure from previous sequence transduction models. Rather than processing tokens in sequential order, the model employs multi-head self-attention mechanisms to capture dependencies across all positions simultaneously. This parallel processing paradigm enables substantial improvements in training efficiency.

The architecture comprises stacked encoder-decoder layers, each containing both self-attention and feed-forward networks. Positional encodings are incorporated to preserve word order information, thereby addressing the inherent position-invariance of the attention mechanism. When evaluated on the WMT 2014 English-German translation task, the transformer achieves 28.4 BLEU, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art by more than 2 BLEU points while requiring substantially less training time."

### Sociolinguistics
**Formal**:
"The corpus comprises 40 sociolinguistic interviews conducted with working-class speakers in Philadelphia. Interview protocols adhered to Labovian methodology, which is designed to elicit natural speech while minimizing observer effects. Each interview was subsequently coded for eight phonological variables across four distinct contextual styles, ranging from casual conversation to minimal pair reading tasks.

Analysis reveals active language change in progress within the community. Younger speakers consistently lead in the adoption of innovative linguistic features, demonstrating significant differences from older cohorts across all variables examined (p < 0.01). This pattern is consistent with generational change rather than age grading, suggesting these features will likely stabilize within the speech community as the younger cohort ages."

### Statistics
**Formal**:
"A mixed-effects logistic regression model was employed, incorporating random intercepts for both subject and item. Fixed effects included experimental condition, trial number, and previous trial accuracy. Model comparison utilizing likelihood ratio tests supported the maximal random effects structure over simpler nested alternatives (χ² = 45.2, df = 3, p < 0.001).

Results demonstrate a significant main effect of experimental condition (β = 0.82, SE = 0.15, p < 0.001), whereby participants in the treatment group exhibited superior performance relative to controls. This effect persisted when potential confounding variables were statistically controlled, thereby suggesting that the intervention causally improves task performance rather than merely correlating with improved outcomes."

## Formal Academic Vocabulary

### Preferred Formal Terms

**Instead of "show"**: demonstrate, reveal, indicate, suggest, evidence

**Instead of "use"**: employ, utilize (when appropriate), implement, apply

**Instead of "get"**: obtain, acquire, achieve, attain

**Instead of "find"**: discover, observe, identify, detect

**Instead of "big/important"**: substantial, significant, considerable, notable, salient

**Instead of "look at"**: examine, investigate, analyze, explore, scrutinize

### Formal Sentence Openers

- "It is noteworthy that..."
- "The present analysis demonstrates..."
- "Previous research has established..."
- "The data suggest..."
- "It should be emphasized that..."
- "Importantly, the findings reveal..."

## Integration with Jargon Density

### With None Jargon
Formal structure + accessible explanations (unusual combination but possible)

"The model processes all positions simultaneously—a technique termed parallel processing—rather than sequentially processing each element in turn. This approach substantially reduces the time required for training while maintaining or improving performance on translation tasks."

### With Selective Jargon
Formal structure + technical vocabulary with glosses

"The model employs multi-head self-attention (parallel attention mechanisms that focus on different aspects of lexical relationships) to process all positions simultaneously. This enables parallel computation, thereby reducing training time from several days to a matter of hours while achieving state-of-the-art performance on benchmark translation tasks."

### With Heavy Jargon
Formal structure + expert terminology (most common pairing)

"The architecture comprises a 12-layer encoder with 8 attention heads per layer and 768-dimensional embeddings. Training was completed in 12 hours on 8 V100 GPUs, achieving 28.4 BLEU on the WMT 2014 English-German translation task, thereby surpassing previous state-of-the-art by 2.1 BLEU points."

## Quality Checklist

Before finalizing synthesis in Formal Structure, verify:

- [ ] No contractions anywhere
- [ ] Passive voice used strategically (40-60% of sentences)
- [ ] Average sentence length 20-30 words
- [ ] Complex sentences with multiple clauses
- [ ] Formal academic transitions throughout
- [ ] Third person exclusively (no "I" or "you")
- [ ] Scholarly vocabulary (demonstrate not show, employ not use)
- [ ] Appropriate for dissertation or publication context
- [ ] Maintains disciplinary conventions
- [ ] Sounds like published academic writing

---

## Common Pitfalls

❌ **Over-reliance on passive**: Every sentence passive, agency obscured
**Fix**: Still use active voice 40-50% of time for clarity

❌ **Unnecessarily archaic**: "Heretofore", "aforementioned", "in light of the foregoing"
**Fix**: Formal doesn't mean archaic—use contemporary academic language

❌ **Purple prose**: Flowery or unnecessarily ornate language
**Fix**: Formal means scholarly, not baroque

❌ **Nominalizations everywhere**: "The implementation of the utilization of..."
**Fix**: Some nominalization is fine, but avoid stacking them

❌ **Losing clarity**: So formal that meaning becomes obscured
**Fix**: Formal should still be clear—complexity serves precision, not obfuscation

## Examples of Good Formal Flow

### Introduction paragraph:

"The question of whether neural language models learn genuine syntactic structure or merely approximate linguistic patterns through statistical regularities remains contested within computational linguistics. The present study addresses this question by probing the internal representations of BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), a widely adopted pre-trained language model.

The methodology employs diagnostic classifiers to predict syntactic properties from BERT's hidden states. Rather than training BERT on explicitly annotated syntactic data, the model's pre-trained representations are held fixed, and simple linear classifiers are trained to predict dependency relations, part-of-speech categories, and hierarchical structural configurations. If such classifiers achieve high accuracy, this suggests that BERT's representations encode syntactic information despite the model never having been explicitly trained on syntactic tasks.

The results demonstrate that BERT's representations do indeed capture hierarchical syntactic structure to a considerable degree. The model demonstrates particular strength in identifying long-range dependencies and structural configurations, substantially outperforming baseline models that rely solely on surface-level cues. While not achieving perfect accuracy, these findings suggest that self-supervised pre-training on large text corpora enables the emergence of syntactic knowledge without explicit supervision."

**What works**:
- No contractions
- Mix of active and passive voice
- Complex sentences with embedded clauses
- Formal transitions: "The methodology employs", "Rather than", "If such"
- Third person throughout
- Scholarly vocabulary: "contested", "employs", "demonstrates"
- Appropriate for dissertation or publication
- Still clear despite formality

## Notes for Implementation

- **Default register for**: Dissertation material, formal publications, traditional academic contexts
- **Tone**: Scholarly, credible, authoritative
- **Readability**: Lower than Mixed or Conversational—requires more effort
- **Credibility**: Highest—signals expertise and academic seriousness

**Remember**: Formal Structure is for when formality itself conveys information—"this is serious scholarly work." Use it when the context demands it, but recognize it's harder to read and slower to write. For most personal notes, Mixed or Conversational will serve you better.

This is the "dissertation chapter" register.
