# Sentence Structure: Mixed

## Strategy
Balance professional clarity with academic conventions. Use both active and passive voice strategically. Moderate sentence complexity with clear logical flow.

**Philosophy**: Professional doesn't require stuffiness. Write clearly and directly while maintaining academic credibility. This is the "safe default" for most contexts.

---

## When to Use This Register

✅ **Use Mixed Structure when:**
- Creating notes for professional contexts
- Sharing with academic peers or advisors
- Want professional tone without formality overhead
- Default choice when unsure (most versatile)
- Literature review notes that may become dissertation material

❌ **Don't use Mixed Structure when:**
- Want maximum accessibility (use Conversational)
- Writing formal submissions (use Formal)
- Personal notes where efficiency matters (use Conversational or Formal depending on preference)

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Minimize But Don't Eliminate Contractions
Use contractions occasionally for flow, but less than conversational register.

**Mixed**: "The authors don't provide explicit justification for this choice, though it's consistent with standard practice in the field."

**Frequency**: ~25% of eligible cases (vs 75%+ for Conversational, 0% for Formal)

**When to use contractions in Mixed**:
- Common verbs: "don't", "can't", "won't", "it's", "there's"
- In subordinate clauses or asides
- When rhythm demands it

**When to avoid**:
- Key claims or findings
- Technical definitions
- Where formality adds weight

### 2. Strategic Voice Usage
Use active voice for clarity, passive voice when appropriate.

**Active voice (60-70%)**: For agency, clarity, engagement
- "The researchers analyzed 40 interviews."
- "Smith argues that syntactic structure emerges from usage."
- "The model achieves state-of-the-art performance."

**Passive voice (30-40%)**: When appropriate
- Established methods: "The data were normalized using standard procedures."
- When agent is obvious/unimportant: "P-values below 0.05 were considered significant."
- For variety and rhythm: "The effect was observed across all age groups."

### 3. Moderate Sentence Complexity
Mix simple and complex sentences. More varied than Conversational, less dense than Formal.

**Simple**: "The model processes all tokens simultaneously."
**Complex**: "The model processes all tokens simultaneously, using multi-head attention to capture relationships between distant words while maintaining computational efficiency."

**Guidelines**:
- Average 15-25 words per sentence
- 1-3 clauses per sentence
- Occasional complex sentence (3+ clauses) for important connections
- Follow complex sentence with simpler one for rhythm

### 4. Professional But Clear Transitions
Use academic transitions but favor clearer, more direct ones.

**Good Mixed transitions**:
- "However" (clear, professional)
- "In contrast" (clear relationship)
- "As a result" (direct causality)
- "For example" (straightforward)
- "Importantly" (signals key point)

**Avoid overly formal**:
- "Notwithstanding"
- "Heretofore"  
- "Aforementioned"
- "In light of the foregoing"

### 5. Third Person Throughout
Maintain scholarly distance. Avoid first person.

**Third person**: "The paper demonstrates...", "The researchers found...", "This suggests...", "The analysis reveals..."

**Not appropriate**: "I argue this connects to...", "This reading suggests...", "The key insight here is..."

*Exception: Second person may occasionally be useful when connecting findings to the user's research context ("This helps address your question about..."), but use sparingly.*

## Examples Across Domains

### Linguistics/Phonetics
**Mixed**:
"The study analyzes /æ/-tensing (vowel raising and fronting) in pre-nasal environments among Chicago speakers. Acoustic measurements of F1 and F2 formants reveal significant age stratification, with younger speakers exhibiting more tensing than older speakers (p < 0.001).

The researchers recorded 40 sociolinguistic interviews and coded tokens for phonological environment, word class, and social factors. The multivariate analysis controls for these variables while isolating the age effect. Results align with previous Northern Cities Vowel Shift research, though Chicago shows more advanced backing of /ɑ/ than Detroit or Buffalo."

### Machine Learning
**Mixed**:
"The transformer architecture represents a significant departure from previous sequence models. Rather than processing tokens sequentially, it uses multi-head self-attention to capture relationships across all positions simultaneously. This parallel processing enables substantial training efficiency gains.

The model consists of stacked encoder-decoder layers, each containing attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks. Positional encodings preserve word order information, addressing the architecture's inherent position-invariance. When trained on WMT 2014 English-German translation, the transformer achieves 28.4 BLEU, outperforming previous state-of-the-art by over 2 points."

### Sociolinguistics
**Mixed**:
"The corpus comprises 40 sociolinguistic interviews conducted with working-class speakers in Philadelphia. Interview protocols followed Labovian methodology designed to elicit natural speech while minimizing observer effects. Each interview was coded for eight phonological variables across four contextual styles, from casual conversation to minimal pair reading tasks.

The analysis reveals active change in progress. Younger speakers lead in most innovative features, showing significant differences from older cohorts (p < 0.01 across all variables). This pattern is consistent with generational change rather than age grading, suggesting these features will likely stabilize in the community."

### Statistics
**Mixed**:
"The mixed-effects regression model includes random intercepts for both subject and item, with fixed effects for condition, trial number, and previous trial accuracy. Model comparison using likelihood ratio tests supports the maximal random effects structure over simpler alternatives (χ² = 45.2, df = 3, p < 0.001).

Results show a significant main effect of condition (β = 0.82, SE = 0.15, p < 0.001), with the treatment group demonstrating higher accuracy than controls. The effect persists when controlling for potential confounds, suggesting the intervention causally improves performance."

## Balancing Accessibility and Professionalism

### Key Tension
Mixed structure navigates between:
- **Accessibility**: Clear enough for non-experts to follow
- **Professionalism**: Credible for academic contexts

### Resolution Strategies

**Use clear academic vocabulary, not obscure synonyms**:
- "analyze" not "interrogate"
- "shows" not "evinces"
- "important" not "salient"

**Explain without condescension**:
- "The F1 formant (first resonance frequency) measures..."
- Not: "The F1 formant, which experts will recognize as..."

**Professional tone without stuffiness**:
- "The results are clear" not "The results unambiguously demonstrate"
- "This suggests" not "This would seem to indicate"

---

## Integration with Jargon Density

### With None Jargon
Professional structure + accessible explanations

"The model processes all positions simultaneously (parallel processing) rather than sequentially, which substantially reduces training time while maintaining or improving performance on translation tasks."

### With Selective Jargon
Professional structure + learning mode

"The model employs multi-head self-attention (parallel attention mechanisms focusing on different aspects of word relationships) to process all positions simultaneously. This enables parallel computation, which reduces training time from days to hours while achieving state-of-the-art performance."

### With Heavy Jargon
Professional structure + expert terminology

"The model utilizes 12-layer encoder architecture with 8 attention heads per layer and 768-dimensional embeddings. Training was completed in 12 hours on 8 GPUs, achieving 28.4 BLEU on English-German translation."

## Quality Checklist

Before finalizing synthesis in Mixed Structure, verify:

- [ ] Active voice used 60-70% of the time
- [ ] Contractions appear occasionally (~25% of eligible cases)
- [ ] Average sentence length 15-25 words
- [ ] Mix of simple and complex sentences for rhythm
- [ ] Professional transitions (however, therefore, as a result)
- [ ] Third person throughout (second person only if connecting to user's goals)
- [ ] No overly formal or archaic phrasing
- [ ] Clear logical flow between ideas
- [ ] Sounds professional without being stuffy
- [ ] Appropriate for sharing with academic colleagues

---

## Common Pitfalls

❌ **Sliding into Formal**: Eliminating all contractions, using only passive voice
**Fix**: Remember Mixed allows contractions and active voice—use them

❌ **Sliding into Conversational**: Too many questions, very short sentences
**Fix**: More variety in sentence length, fewer rhetorical questions

❌ **Inconsistent voice**: Switching between conversational and formal within paragraph
**Fix**: Maintain steady professional-but-clear tone throughout

❌ **Overusing passive**: "It was found that...", "The data were analyzed..."
**Fix**: Active voice for majority; passive only when warranted

❌ **Unnecessarily formal vocabulary**: "utilize" for "use", "commence" for "start"
**Fix**: Choose clear words over impressive-sounding ones

## Examples of Good Mixed Flow

### Results paragraph:

"The acoustic analysis reveals significant /æ/-tensing in pre-nasal environments. F1 measurements show an average lowering of 85 Hz in tensed versus lax tokens (t = 8.4, p < 0.001, d = 1.2). This effect persists across different phonological contexts, though it is strongest before /n/ and /m/.

Age patterns demonstrate clear stratification. Younger speakers exhibit substantially more tensing than older speakers, with the effect increasing by approximately 12 Hz per decade (β = 12, SE = 2.1, p < 0.001). This suggests active change in progress rather than stable variation. The pattern aligns with broader Northern Cities Vowel Shift trajectories documented in Detroit and Buffalo."

**What works**:
- Mix of simple and complex sentences
- Active and passive voice both present
- Professional transitions: "This suggests", "The pattern aligns"
- One contraction: "it is" → "it's" would work, "it is" maintains professional tone
- Clear logical flow
- Technical detail without stuffiness
- Could share with advisor or peer without revision

## Notes for Implementation

- **Default register for**: Most professional academic contexts
- **Tone**: Professional but accessible, credible but clear
- **Versatility**: Works across widest range of contexts
- **Safety**: When unsure which register to use, Mixed is safest choice

**Remember**: Mixed Structure is the workhorse register. It's professional enough for academic contexts while remaining clear and readable. Use Conversational when you want warmth, Formal when you need maximum credibility—but Mixed handles most situations well.

This is the "I might share this with my advisor" register.
