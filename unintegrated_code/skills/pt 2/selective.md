# Jargon Density: Selective

## Strategy
Use technical terminology as primary language with inline glosses for specialized terms. Keep precise jargon while building reader's vocabulary through exposure.

**Philosophy**: Technical terms are the *right* words for concepts—don't avoid them, but don't assume familiarity either. Gloss specialized terms inline so reader learns field vocabulary while maintaining precision.

---

## When to Use This Register

✅ **Use Selective Jargon when:**
- Reader is learning the field but has some background
- Recovering from hiatus and refreshing vocabulary
- Creating notes for your future self to reference
- Building vocabulary through repeated exposure
- Want portable notes (precise enough for experts, accessible enough for motivated learners)

❌ **Don't use Selective Jargon when:**
- Reader is complete beginner (use None)
- Reader is field expert needing efficient review (use Heavy)
- Speed matters more than learning
- Audience mix is very wide (use None for accessibility)

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Technical Terms as Primary Language
Use field-appropriate jargon as the main terminology. Don't shy away from precision.

**Good**: "The study analyzes alveolar fricatives in word-final position."
(Then gloss what reader might not know)

**Not**: "The study looks at hissing sounds made near the tooth ridge at the end of words."
(Too accessible—loses precision)

### 2. Inline Glosses for Specialized Terms
Gloss field-specific concepts that require prior coursework or deep familiarity.

**Format**: `technical-term (accessible explanation)`

**What to gloss**:
- Specialized terminology not in general academic vocabulary
- Concepts requiring domain-specific coursework
- Abbreviations/acronyms on first use
- Methodology-specific terms

**What NOT to gloss**:
- General academic words: hypothesis, significant, methodology, corpus
- Terms defined in previous section
- Common abbreviations after first use
- Widely-known concepts in the field

### 3. Progressive Vocabulary Building
Gloss on first use, then use freely. Trust reader to remember.

**First mention**:
"The model uses multi-head self-attention (mechanism where different 'attention heads' focus on different aspects of relationships between words) to capture complex patterns."

**Later mentions**:
"Each attention head specializes in different syntactic relationships."
"The self-attention mechanism scales efficiently to long sequences."

### 4. Expand Acronyms on First Use
Spell out + brief gloss, then use acronym freely.

**Examples**:
- "VOT (Voice Onset Time—delay between consonant release and vowel start)"
- "BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers—model that reads text in both directions)"
- "ANOVA (Analysis of Variance—statistical test comparing multiple groups)"

## Examples Across Domains

### Linguistics/Phonetics
"Participants exhibited /æ/-tensing (vowel becomes tighter and higher in the mouth) in pre-nasal environments (before sounds like 'n' or 'm'). This pattern is consistent with other Northern Cities Vowel Shift features."

**Note**: Gloss /æ/-tensing and pre-nasal, assume reader knows "vowel shift" from context.

### Machine Learning
"The transformer architecture employs multi-head self-attention (parallel attention mechanisms that focus on different aspects of input relationships) with positional encoding (markers indicating word position in sequence). Unlike RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks—models that process sequences step by step), transformers process all tokens simultaneously."

**Note**: Gloss complex mechanisms, assume reader knows "architecture," "tokens," "sequence."

### Sociolinguistics
"The corpus comprises 40 sociolinguistic interviews (recorded conversations designed to elicit natural speech) from working-class informants. Interview protocol followed Labov's methodology for capturing vernacular style (everyday speech rather than careful formal register)."

**Note**: Gloss specialized methods, assume reader knows "corpus," "informants," "protocol."

### Statistics
"Results showed p < 0.05 (less than 5% chance the pattern is due to random chance), with effect sizes ranging from d = 0.3 to d = 0.8 (Cohen's d—standardized measure where 0.3 is small-medium, 0.8 is large). The 95% confidence intervals suggest the true population effect is substantial."

**Note**: Gloss p-values and effect sizes, assume reader knows "confidence intervals."

### Critical Theory
"The text exemplifies epistemic violence (harm done by excluding certain ways of knowing) through hegemonic discourse (culturally dominant ways of talking that feel natural rather than imposed). This operates through what Foucault termed power/knowledge (the idea that power and knowledge produce each other)."

**Note**: Gloss theoretical concepts, assume reader knows "discourse," "operates through."

## Gloss Format Guidelines

### Standard Inline Gloss
`term (brief accessible explanation)`

**Examples**:
- "alveolar fricative (hissing sound like 's' or 'z' made near tooth ridge)"
- "code-switching (alternating between languages in conversation)"
- "multivariate regression (statistical method analyzing multiple variables simultaneously)"

### Acronym Gloss
`ACRONYM (Full Name—brief explanation of what it does)`

**Examples**:
- "ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit—activation function that outputs input if positive, zero otherwise)"
- "fMRI (functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging—brain scan showing activity patterns)"

### Contrastive Gloss
When distinguishing from similar concepts:

"Unlike RNNs (which process sequences step by step), transformers use self-attention (processing all positions simultaneously to learn relationships)."

### Relational Gloss
When term builds on previous concept:

"The encoder (component that reads and processes input) feeds into the decoder (component that generates output), connected through cross-attention (mechanism allowing decoder to focus on relevant encoder states)."

## Integration with Output Tasks

### Quick Summaries
- Use technical terminology in summary
- Gloss 3-5 core specialized terms
- Expand acronyms on first use
- Assume general academic vocabulary is known

### Detailed Summaries
- Gloss new specialized terms in each section
- Build vocabulary progressively through sections
- Track which terms have been introduced
- More glosses in methodology, fewer in results/discussion

### Glossary Extraction
- Terms already identified through glossing
- Extract glosses as starting definitions
- Expand with examples and context
- Cross-reference related concepts

## Quality Checklist

Before finalizing synthesis in Selective Jargon register, verify:

- [ ] Technical terminology used as primary language
- [ ] All specialized/field-specific terms glossed on first use
- [ ] All acronyms expanded on first use with brief explanation
- [ ] No glosses for general academic vocabulary
- [ ] Terms glossed once, then used freely
- [ ] Glosses are brief (one phrase or sentence)
- [ ] Reader builds vocabulary through exposure
- [ ] Maintains precision while supporting learning

---

## Common Pitfalls

❌ **Over-glossing**: Glossing every technical term including common ones
**Fix**: Reserve glosses for truly specialized concepts

❌ **Re-glossing**: Explaining the same term multiple times
**Fix**: Gloss once on first use, track what's been introduced

❌ **Too-brief glosses**: One-word synonym that doesn't clarify
**Fix**: Provide enough context to actually explain the concept

❌ **Glosses that are mini-essays**: Breaking flow with long asides
**Fix**: Keep glosses to one phrase or sentence; save detail for glossary

❌ **Avoiding technical terms**: Using accessible language throughout
**Fix**: This is Selective, not None—use the technical terms!

## Examples of Good Selective Jargon Flow

### Opening paragraph with progressive glossing:

"This paper investigates phonological variation (differences in pronunciation patterns) in Chicago English using a corpus of sociolinguistic interviews (recorded conversations designed to elicit natural speech). The focus is on /æ/-tensing (when the vowel in 'cat' becomes tighter and higher) in pre-nasal environments (before sounds like 'n' or 'm'). Previous research on the Northern Cities Vowel Shift has documented similar patterns in Detroit and Buffalo, but Chicago data shows interesting conditioning factors.

The researchers employ acoustic analysis to measure F1 and F2 formants (vocal tract resonances that determine vowel quality—F1 relates to tongue height, F2 to frontness/backness). They use multivariate regression to test whether tensing correlates with social factors like age, gender, and neighborhood. Results show significant age grading, with younger speakers showing more tensing than older speakers (p < 0.05)."

**What works**:
- Technical terms used freely after glossing
- Specialized concepts glossed, common terms assumed
- Flow isn't broken by excessive glossing
- Reader learns 5-6 new terms while following argument
- Progressive building: F1/F2 explained, then "formants" used alone later

## Notes for Implementation

- **Default register for**: Personal reading notes, refresher after hiatus, learning mode
- **Word count**: Moderate (glosses add some length but not as much as None)
- **Tone**: Professional but pedagogical—teaching while informing
- **Pacing**: Measured—allow time for vocabulary building without hand-holding

**Remember**: Selective Jargon builds vocabulary through exposure. Each gloss is a mini-lesson. Over time, reader needs fewer glosses as terms become familiar. The goal is to graduate from Selective → Heavy as field knowledge solidifies.

This is the DEFAULT mode for most alcanzai use cases.
