# Understand Academic Text

## Purpose

Parse and comprehend scholarly papers across disciplines. This skill provides the foundational cognitive framework for reading academic work: identifying structure, understanding argumentation, and extracting key information.

**Use this skill when:** Processing any academic paper, article, or scholarly text regardless of field.

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Academic Papers Have Predictable Structure

Even across disciplines, papers follow recognizable patterns:
- **Sciences/STEM:** Often IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion)
- **Humanities:** Usually Thesis → Evidence → Analysis → Conclusion
- **Social Sciences:** Mix of both, often Introduction → Theory → Data → Analysis

Understanding structure helps you know WHERE to look for WHAT information.

### 2. Papers Build Arguments Through Layers

Academic writing constructs arguments through:
- **Claims** (what the author asserts)
- **Evidence** (data, citations, examples supporting claims)
- **Reasoning** (logical connections between evidence and claims)
- **Qualifications** (limitations, caveats, scope)

Your job: identify these layers explicitly.

### 3. Context Matters

Papers exist in conversation with prior work:
- What problem does this solve?
- What gap does this fill?
- How does this relate to other research?
- Who is the intended audience?

---

## Reading Process Checklist

Copy and complete this checklist when analyzing a paper:

### Phase 1: Survey the Landscape
- [ ] Read title and abstract
- [ ] Scan section headers to understand structure
- [ ] Note paper length (signals depth of treatment)
- [ ] Identify publication venue (journal/conference → field conventions)
- [ ] Check publication date (recency matters for fast-moving fields)
- [ ] Scan references (who do they cite → intellectual lineage)

### Phase 2: Identify the Core Argument
- [ ] What is the main claim or contribution?
- [ ] What problem are they solving?
- [ ] What gap in knowledge are they filling?
- [ ] What is novel or surprising about their approach?
- [ ] What would someone in this field care about?

### Phase 3: Understand the Structure
- [ ] Map major sections and their purposes
- [ ] Identify where key claims are made (usually intro + conclusion)
- [ ] Identify where evidence is presented (methods, results, case studies)
- [ ] Note transitions between sections (how do they build on each other?)
- [ ] Flag any non-standard organization (why did they structure it this way?)

### Phase 4: Extract Key Information
- [ ] Main findings or conclusions
- [ ] Methodology (how did they do the work?)
- [ ] Evidence base (what supports their claims?)
- [ ] Limitations acknowledged by authors
- [ ] Future work or open questions
- [ ] Technical terminology introduced

### Phase 5: Situate in Context
- [ ] What prior work do they build on?
- [ ] What do they critique or challenge?
- [ ] Who is the intended audience (experts? general readers?)
- [ ] What assumptions do they make about reader knowledge?
- [ ] How does this fit into broader conversations in the field?

## Common Paper Structures

### IMRaD (Sciences, Engineering, Medicine)

**Introduction**
- Motivation: Why does this matter?
- Background: What do we already know?
- Gap: What's missing in current knowledge?
- Contribution: What does this paper add?

**Methods**
- Experimental design or analytical approach
- Data collection procedures
- Tools, instruments, or software used
- Statistical or analytical techniques

**Results**
- Findings presented objectively
- Tables, figures, statistical tests
- Raw observations without interpretation

**Discussion**
- Interpretation of results
- Connection to prior work
- Implications for the field
- Limitations and caveats
- Future directions

### Humanities/Theory Papers

**Introduction/Thesis**
- Central argument or interpretive claim
- Theoretical framework being used
- Texts or phenomena being analyzed
- Stakes: Why does this reading matter?

**Background/Literature Review**
- Prior interpretations or scholarship
- Theoretical foundations
- Historical or cultural context

**Analysis (often multiple sections)**
- Close reading of primary texts
- Application of theoretical framework
- Evidence supporting interpretation
- Engagement with counter-arguments

**Conclusion**
- Synthesis of argument
- Broader implications
- Open questions or new interpretations

### Social Sciences (Mixed)

**Introduction**
- Research question or hypothesis
- Theoretical framework
- Significance of the question

**Literature Review/Theory**
- Prior research on the topic
- Theoretical approach guiding analysis
- Conceptual framework

**Methods/Data**
- Research design (qualitative, quantitative, mixed)
- Data collection (surveys, interviews, archival, etc.)
- Analytical approach

**Analysis/Findings**
- Presentation of data
- Patterns or themes identified
- Statistical or interpretive analysis

**Discussion/Conclusion**
- Interpretation of findings
- Connection to theory and prior work
- Policy or practical implications
- Limitations

## Identifying Arguments vs Evidence vs Speculation

Use this framework to categorize statements:

### Claims (Assertions)
**Signals:**
- "We argue that..."
- "This demonstrates..."
- "Our findings show..."
- "We propose..."

**Example:** "Transformer models develop implicit syntactic representations without explicit supervision."

### Evidence (Support)
**Signals:**
- "The data reveal..."
- "Table 2 shows..."
- "Participants reported..."
- "As Smith (2020) found..."

**Example:** "Probing experiments showed 85% accuracy on syntactic tasks (Table 3)."

### Reasoning (Connecting Evidence to Claims)
**Signals:**
- "This suggests..."
- "Therefore..."
- "Because of this..."
- "Given that..."

**Example:** "Because attention heads specialize for different syntactic relations, this suggests implicit hierarchical structure."

### Speculation (Possibilities)
**Signals:**
- "We hypothesize..."
- "This might indicate..."
- "One possibility is..."
- "Future work could..."

**Example:** "These patterns might indicate emergence of symbolic reasoning, though further investigation is needed."

## Handling Technical Terminology

When encountering unfamiliar terms:

### Step 1: Try Context First
- Can you infer meaning from surrounding sentences?
- Is it defined in the text (look for parenthetical explanations)?
- Is there a pattern in how it's used across the paper?

### Step 2: Check for Definitions
- Abstract often defines key terms
- Introduction may include background on terminology
- Footnotes or endnotes might provide glosses
- References to prior work may indicate standard usage

### Step 3: Note for Glossing
- Mark terms that seem central to the paper's argument
- Distinguish between:
  - **Field-standard terms** (need to learn these)
  - **Paper-specific terms** (coined by authors)
  - **Borrowed terms** (from other fields)

## Red Flags and Special Cases

### When Structure is Non-Standard

Some papers intentionally break conventions:

**Provocative/Polemic pieces:**
- Argument-driven rather than evidence-driven
- May skip traditional "objective" tone
- Focus on critique or alternative perspectives

**Response:** Note this is a different genre, adjust expectations

**Interdisciplinary work:**
- May blend conventions from multiple fields
- Section headers might not map to standard IMRaD
- May need to explain concepts from multiple domains

**Response:** Identify which field conventions are being mixed

**Methodological/theoretical papers:**
- May lack empirical results section
- Focus on conceptual frameworks or new methods
- Argument is about approach rather than findings

**Response:** Treat the method/theory itself as the "result"

### When You're Missing Context

If you can't fully understand the paper because of missing background:

- [ ] Identify what prerequisite knowledge you're missing
- [ ] Note this explicitly (don't pretend to understand)
- [ ] Focus on what you CAN understand (structure, approach, conclusions)
- [ ] Flag for the reader: "This builds on X which requires background in Y"

## Examples Across Disciplines

### Example 1: STEM Paper (Machine Learning)

**Paper:** "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al. 2017)

**Structure identified:**
- Introduction: Neural translation needs better models than RNNs
- Background: Review of sequence-to-sequence models
- Model Architecture: Detailed description of Transformer
- Experiments: Translation tasks, benchmarks
- Results: Performance metrics, comparisons
- Analysis: Why it works (attention visualization)
- Conclusion: Implications for broader NLP

**Main argument:** 
Self-attention mechanisms alone (without recurrence or convolution) are sufficient for state-of-the-art sequence transduction.

**Evidence:**
- BLEU scores on WMT translation tasks
- Training speed comparisons
- Attention weight visualizations

**Key terminology to gloss:**
- Multi-head attention
- Positional encoding
- Self-attention
- Encoder-decoder architecture

### Example 2: Humanities Paper (Critical Theory)

**Paper:** "Panopticism" (Foucault, Discipline and Punish)

**Structure identified:**
- Opening: Description of plague town regulations (historical example)
- Analysis: Disciplinary mechanisms in plague response
- Transition: From plague to panopticon (Bentham's prison design)
- Deep Analysis: How panopticon represents modern power
- Broader Implications: Disciplinary society beyond prisons
- Conclusion: Surveillance as normalization

**Main argument:**
The panopticon represents a shift in power from sovereign (spectacular punishment) to disciplinary (internalized surveillance).

**Evidence:**
- Historical texts about plague regulations
- Bentham's prison architecture writings
- Examples from schools, hospitals, factories

**Key terminology to gloss:**
- Panopticon
- Disciplinary power
- Surveillance
- Normalization

### Example 3: Social Science (Sociolinguistics)

**Paper:** "The Social Stratification of (r) in New York City" (Labov, 1966)

**Structure identified:**
- Introduction: Research question about /r/ pronunciation
- Background: Prior work on linguistic variation
- Methodology: Department store study design
- Data: Three stores (high, mid, low prestige)
- Analysis: /r/ usage correlated with store prestige
- Discussion: Social meaning of linguistic variables
- Conclusion: Language reflects social stratification

**Main argument:**
Pronunciation patterns systematically correlate with social class, demonstrating language as social practice.

**Evidence:**
- Recordings from store employees
- Statistical analysis of /r/ presence
- Correlation with store prestige ranking

**Key terminology to gloss:**
- Sociolinguistic variable
- Style shifting
- Social stratification
- Linguistic prestige

## Self-Check: Am I Using This Skill Well?

After processing a paper, ask:

✅ **Structure:** Can I name the major sections and their purposes?
✅ **Argument:** Can I state the main claim in one sentence?
✅ **Evidence:** Can I identify what supports the main claim?
✅ **Context:** Do I know what gap this fills or problem it solves?
✅ **Terminology:** Have I flagged key terms for glossing?
✅ **Audience:** Do I understand who would care about this work?

If you can't answer these, you need to re-read with more attention to structure and argument.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

❌ **Summarizing chronologically rather than argumentatively**
- Don't just list what happens in each section
- Focus on how sections build toward the argument

❌ **Treating all claims equally**
- Main thesis ≠ supporting point ≠ background fact
- Emphasize what's novel or central

❌ **Ignoring qualifications and limitations**
- Authors usually acknowledge scope and caveats
- These are important for understanding claims properly

❌ **Skipping the context**
- Papers don't exist in isolation
- Understanding prior work helps you see what's new

❌ **Over-relying on abstract**
- Abstracts are compressed and sometimes misleading
- Read the full introduction and conclusion

5. **Connect to other skills** - This is a foundation, not standalone
